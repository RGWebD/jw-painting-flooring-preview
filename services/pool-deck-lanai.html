Pool deck resurfacing in Jacksonville: what the job covers

A deck coating is a thin layer bonded to the concrete you already have. Done right, it hides stains and old patches, adds grip and runs cooler than a dark or worn slab. Laid over dirt or moving cracks, it peels within a season or two. We coat three kinds of outdoor space. Open pool decks take the most sun, chlorine and splash. Patios carry grills, planters and furniture legs. Screened or roofed lanais get less direct sun, which opens up more finish choices, including decorative flake.

Prep is the bigger share of the work. JW is also a pressure-washing company, so the crew that cleans the deck is the crew that coats it. Every job begins with a measure and a written quote listing the prep, the repairs and the finish. Every deck job starts with a full pressure wash. What we look for Why do pool decks crack, fade and turn slick?

Concrete moves. It swells and shrinks with heat, and Concrete Network’s pool deck guide notes that a slab without properly placed joints cannot expand and contract, so it cracks instead. The deck and the pool shell also move at different rates, which is why the coping joint often opens first. Sun is the next problem. Constant UV discolors every type of epoxy over time, so plain epoxy makes a poor top layer on an open deck. Color matters too: Berkeley Lab’s Heat Island Group explains that dark surfaces absorb almost all of the sun’s energy and heat up.

Water finishes the job. Shaded corners stay damp after summer storms and grow algae that turns slippery. Around saltwater pools, sealer makers warn that salt crystals forming as splash dries can flake unsealed concrete and pavers. Signs your deck is due for work: Hairline cracks that have widened or now run across the deck Old paint that peels, chalks or rubs off on bare feet Green or black growth in the shade and along the coping

Puddles that stand for hours after a rain

From pressure washer to final topcoat, step by step

A deck coating is only as good as the bond underneath it. These are the steps we follow, and the cure times on the product sheet set the pace.

Measure and inspect

We measure the deck and note drains, steps, coping and any screen-enclosure posts. We tap for hollow patches and ask about your pool chemistry, because salt systems and chlorine both shape the finish we recommend.

Pressure wash and dry

A surface cleaner lifts dirt, algae and loose coating, and stubborn growth gets a cleaner and a rinse. Washing alone cannot remove paint or oil, so those spots get extra work. The slab then dries fully before repairs start.

Open the surface

Smooth or previously sealed concrete is ground or etched so the new coat can grip, the same idea behind the surface profiles in ICRI Guideline 310.2R. Failing old paint comes off rather than getting buried.

Repair cracks and joints

Cracks are opened with a crack chaser, filled with a flexible compound and dusted with sand so the next layer bonds. Moving cracks may need a new control joint, and the coping joint gets flexible sealant rather than rigid patch.

Prime, texture and topcoat

Primer goes on first, then a texture coat that is sprayed and knocked down or troweled, then a pigmented acrylic or UV-stable topcoat. Non-skid grit goes into the top layer, heavier on steps and at the water’s edge.

Cure and final walk

Foot traffic stays off for the time the product sheet lists. Then we walk the deck with you, checking texture, edges and joints, and leave the product names and simple washing advice for your records. Cracks are opened, filled and sanded before any coat.

What moves the price on deck and lanai coatings

Square footage sets the base, but condition sets the real number. A sound deck that needs a wash and a fresh topcoat is a short job. A deck with peeling paint, wide cracks and broken edges needs grinding and repair before any coating goes on. The system comes next. A sprayed acrylic texture usually costs less than a full flake broadcast under a polyaspartic topcoat, and a cementitious overlay that rebuilds a rough surface costs more than either. Steps, coping, drains and enclosure posts all add hand work.

Access and timing count too. Furniture, planters and grills have to move, and a lanai inside a screen cage is slower to stage than an open patio. Your written quote lists each line, so you can see how much goes to prep, repairs and the finish itself.

Permits, pool rules and licensing

Florida jurisdictions generally treat painting and similar finish work as exempt from building permits, as the building official decides. New slabs, enlarged decks and structural repairs are different, so confirm with your building department, and check with your HOA if deck colors need approval. Pools at hotels, apartments and some condo and HOA communities fall under Chapter 514, Florida Statutes, and Department of Health oversight. The Florida Building Code’s public pool section calls for wet decks of concrete or other nonabsorbent material with a slip-resistant finish. Florida law also bars local license requirements for painting and pressure-washing work.
