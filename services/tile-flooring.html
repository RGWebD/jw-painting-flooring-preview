Porcelain and ceramic tile floors, set for Jacksonville slabs

Porcelain is not just a fancier name for ceramic. Under ANSI A137.1, porcelain is ceramic tile that absorbs 0.5% of its weight in water or less, tested by ASTM C373. Tile certified by the Porcelain Tile Certification Agency carries a mark saying so. That low absorption is why porcelain suits entries, baths and rooms beside the pool door. Ceramic tile still works well in dry rooms and on a budget. For glazed tile of either kind, check the wear class on the box. Those classes trace back to ASTM C1027, a test of how well a glaze resists visible abrasion.

We set the sizes people want now: wood-look porcelain planks, 24-inch squares and larger. Bigger tile needs a flatter floor and more care at the edges, and the prep below is written around that. If you only have a few broken tiles, tile repair is the better fit than a new floor.

Porcelain is defined by low water absorption, not by looks. What we look for Why does tile tent, crack or sound hollow over concrete?

Concrete and tile both grow and shrink with heat and moisture, at different rates. If the tile has no place to relieve that stress, it pushes against itself until a row lifts off the slab in a tent. Tile grouted hard against the walls, with no soft joint at the edge, is the classic setup. The TCNA Handbook’s EJ171 detail calls for movement joints at the perimeter and every change of plane. In the field, joints go roughly every 20 to 25 feet each way, tightening to 8 to 12 feet where tile gets direct sun or moisture. A tiled great room facing a sliding door falls in the tighter range.

Thin mortar coverage is the other cause. A tile with voids beneath it rings hollow, then cracks under a dropped pan. Signs that a tile floor is already in trouble: A hollow, drummy sound when you tap tiles with a coin A low ridge forming down a hallway or across a doorway Cracks running through several tiles in a straight line

Grout crumbling along one line while the rest stays sound

From bare concrete to finished grout: the tile sequence

Tile is unforgiving of a skipped step, because every flaw is locked under a hard surface. This is the order we hold to.

Sound and map the slab

After tear-out we tap, look and straightedge the bare concrete. Cracks are marked in chalk so the membrane plan follows them. Moisture gets checked too, since a wet slab affects membrane and mortar choices.

Plan the joints

Before any mortar is mixed, we draw the layout with centered rooms and balanced cuts at doors. Movement joints are marked at the perimeter and in the field where EJ171 calls for them.

Flatten for the tile size

ANSI A108.02 asks for a flatter floor once any tile edge reaches 15 inches: no more than 1/8 inch in 10 feet. We grind high spots and pour self-leveler in low ones instead of building up mortar.

Lay the membrane

A crack-isolation membrane meeting ANSI A118.12 goes over cracks or the whole floor. Over a moving slab, an uncoupling mat lets the slab shift without dragging the tile along.

Set and check coverage

Tile is back-buttered and set in thin-set. We lift tiles as we go to confirm mortar contact: ANSI A108.5 calls for at least 80% in dry rooms and 95% in showers and wet areas.

Grout, seal and check lippage

Joints are grouted and the movement joints filled with flexible sealant, never grout. Then we check edge height between tiles with a straightedge and walk the floor with you before we leave. Tiles are lifted during the set to confirm mortar contact.

What changes the cost of a tile floor

Tile size and material move the price first. Large-format and wood-look planks take more flattening and more careful setting than 12-inch squares. Porcelain is denser and harder to cut than ceramic, so cuts around cabinets, columns and doorways take longer. The slab sets the prep bill. A floor that needs grinding and self-leveler to reach the 1/8-inch tolerance costs more than one that is already flat. Old tile in a thick mortar bed is heavy, slow demolition compared with pulling carpet.

Membranes add material and labor, either over isolated cracks or across the whole floor. Patterns matter: herringbone and diagonal layouts mean more cuts and waste than a straight lay. Thresholds, stair nosings, baseboard reset and moving furniture each show up as their own line on the written quote, so nothing is hidden in a lump sum.
