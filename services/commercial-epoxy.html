Commercial epoxy

Commercial epoxy flooring in Jacksonville, matched to how the floor is used

A business floor is a system, not one coat of paint. It starts with a ground and repaired slab, then a primer, a body coat sized to the traffic, and a topcoat picked for chemicals, abrasion or sun. The right build for a dry storage aisle is the wrong one for a dish room. We size the system to the work done on it. Warehouses usually get a thick epoxy with a hard-wearing topcoat. Kitchens often need urethane cement or a textured build that handles hot water. Auto bays need resistance to oils and fluids, and clinic floors have to survive disinfectants and constant mopping.

Warehouses and distribution space. Northeast Florida holds nearly 100 million square feet of warehouse distribution space, according to a Cushman & Wakefield count reported by JAXPORT in 2021. Forklift wheels grind dust out of bare concrete and chip thin coatings at dock edges and turns, so these floors need build and a tough topcoat.

Restaurants and commercial kitchens. Grease, dropped pans and hot-water cleaning wear down standard epoxy over time. Coating makers point wet kitchens toward urethane cement, which goes on thicker and copes better with sudden temperature swings.

Auto shops and service bays. Motor oil, brake fluid and coolant soak into bare concrete and stay. A chemical-resistant topcoat keeps spills on the surface, where a squeegee can reach them.

Clinics and offices. Waiting rooms and exam areas get mopped with disinfectants every day. A jointless resin floor with a cove base leaves no grout lines for grime to settle into.

How an after-hours commercial install works

Most businesses cannot close for a week. We plan the work in zones and shifts so the space keeps running, and we confirm every cure time with you before the first night.

Walk the space and test the slab

We map traffic lanes, drains, fixed equipment and damaged areas. Then we test moisture by the method the coating maker names, such as ASTM F2170 in-situ probes or the ASTM F1869 calcium chloride test.

Plan the phases

The quote splits the floor into zones, each with a start time and a return-to-service time. Fast-curing polyaspartic topcoats can shorten a closure where the product data sheet allows it.

Grind and repair

Diamond grinders with dust extraction open the concrete to the surface profile described in ICRI Guideline 310.2R. Cracks, joints and spalls get filled so they do not show through the finish.

Build the coating

Primer, body coat and topcoat go down at the specified thickness, with aggregate broadcast wherever traction matters. Cove base, safety colors and striping are added in the same sequence.

Cure, inspect and hand back

We check the finish, pull our barriers and walk each zone with you before it reopens. Your staff gets the product names and a cleaning routine that will not dull the topcoat. Grinding opens the slab so the primer can bond.

Cost factors for business floors

Square footage sets the baseline, but the system moves the number more. A thin topcoat in a showroom costs far less per foot than a urethane cement build in a kitchen. Thickness counts as well: a full aggregate broadcast uses more resin and labor than two rolled coats. Prep is the next lever. Old coatings, adhesive, oil-soaked concrete and wide cracks all add grinding and repair time. A slab that tests high for moisture needs a vapor-control primer, which adds material and a step.

Scheduling matters too. Night and weekend phases, short reopening windows and work around fixed equipment take more labor than an empty building. Moving racks or kitchen lines, cove base and striping each get their own line, so the written quote shows exactly where the money goes.
