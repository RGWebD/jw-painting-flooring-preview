Water-damaged floors

Water-damaged floor replacement in Jacksonville, after the drying is done

Every flooded floor goes through two separate jobs. The first is water mitigation: extraction, fans and dehumidifiers, and mold work where it is needed. A restoration company working to the IICRC S500 water-damage standard handles that part. The second job is the rebuild, and that is where our crew comes in. We start when moisture readings show the slab or wood subfloor is dry. Then we pull whatever flooring the drying crew left, scrape the old adhesive, correct low or damaged spots, and install the new floor. That might be vinyl plank , porcelain tile or a coated slab. Boards that only lifted or cupped may be worth saving through buckled floor repair instead.

If your house sits in a flood zone, we go over the materials FEMA treats as flood damage-resistant before you pick. Putting back exactly what was there is not always smart when another storm season opens on June 1. A moisture barrier goes over the slab before the new plank. What we look for Can the floor dry in place, or does it have to come out?

It depends on the water and the material. Under IICRC S500, a burst pipe or failed supply line starts as Category 1, clean water. A washer or dishwasher overflow is Category 2. Sewage, sea water and rising river water are Category 3. A Florida Department of Community Affairs fact sheet adds that time and heat can degrade even clean water. That same fact sheet lists carpet, pad, sheet vinyl and tile floor materials among the coverings to pull and discard in cleanup. It also warns that non-porous layers can trap moisture and slow drying, and that flooring adhesives give mold something to feed on. Floating plank over a slab is a common trap: the top looks fine while water sits underneath.

Tile set in thin-set on concrete often survives, which is why FEMA counts that assembly as flood damage-resistant. Even so, tile can let go of the slab afterward. The order of work once the restoration crew leaves

A new floor laid on a damp slab fails a second time. These steps keep the replacement from repeating the damage.

Tell us what happened

Call with the water source, the rooms involved and whether drying has started. If no one is drying the house yet, get a water-mitigation company in first. That work cannot wait for a flooring quote.

Confirm the floor is dry

We read wood subfloors with a moisture meter and test concrete the way the new product requires, often with ASTM F2170 in-situ humidity probes. Nothing new goes down until readings sit inside the maker’s limits.

Document for the claim

We photograph each room, measure it and note product names from any leftover boxes. A cut sample of the old floor goes to you, since FloodSmart suggests keeping material samples for the adjuster. The written quote itemizes tear-out, prep and materials.

Tear out and prepare

Remaining flooring comes up and adhesive is scraped. Ridges get ground flat and dips get self-leveler. If plywood is soft or joists look damaged, we stop and point you to a contractor who handles framing.

Install the replacement

Vinyl plank goes over a moisture barrier on the slab. Tile goes over a crack-isolation membrane where the concrete has hairline cracks. Each product follows its own installation guide.

Trim, touch up and walk through

Baseboards, transitions and thresholds go back on, and scuffed paint gets touched up. We walk the rooms with you and leave the product names for your records and your claim file. Nothing new goes down until the readings are inside the maker’s limits.

Cost factors on a water-damage floor job

Square footage is the starting point, but the condition of the house after drying often matters more. If the restoration crew already pulled carpet and pad, there is less to remove. Glue-down plank and tile set in mortar take longer to strip than a floating floor. The slab or subfloor is the next variable. Hairline cracks, dips left by old mortar and soft plywood near a toilet or dishwasher each add prep time. Then comes the material you choose, and whether undamaged rooms must be redone so one floor runs through the house. Tile set in thin-set takes more labor than floating plank, but it is the assembly FEMA treats as flood damage-resistant.

Pulling and resetting toilets, appliances and base trim adds labor too. Every item appears as a separate line on the written quote, so you and your adjuster can read the same numbers.
